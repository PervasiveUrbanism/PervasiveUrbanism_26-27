## Slide 8 — Revealing

We start the year with a phase we call *Revealing*.

Before we begin designing anything, we want to understand the city and the different ways in which people experience it.

A city is not experienced in the same way by everyone. The same street, square or neighbourhood can mean very different things to different people. Some places feel welcoming, others not so.

We find these differences very interesting and we want you to investigate these differences and make them visible.

You will work in groups of three or four and develop your own research question. In the skill module we will introduce a range of computational tools to help you collect, analyse and visualise information, including data from digital platforms, archives, images and other sources.

The outcome will be what we call *Cartographies of Affect*: maps that go beyond the physical layout of the city and reveal its social, cultural and emotional dimensions.

Importantly, we don't expect you to arrive at a design proposal immediately. We want you to develop a strong research question that can guide your work throughout the year.

## Earlier draft — Slide 9: Cartographies of Affect — Making the Invisible Visible

Here you can see some examples of what we mean by Cartographies of Affect.

These are not conventional maps showing buildings, streets or infrastructure. Instead, they attempt to visualise aspects of the city that are normally difficult to see.

For example, we might investigate how people feel about particular places, how different communities use public space, or how certain areas are represented and discussed online.

The interesting thing is that we can take information that is usually scattered across many different sources and bring it together into a spatial representation.

This allows us to discover patterns, relationships and sometimes contradictions that would otherwise remain hidden.

And importantly, there isn't one correct way to make these maps. The way you represent your findings should emerge from the question you are investigating.

## Earlier draft — Slide 10: Cartographies of Affect — Beyond Conventional Mapping

These examples also demonstrate that a map doesn't necessarily have to look like a traditional geographical map.

It can be a diagram, a network, a timeline or a more experimental visualisation. It might combine geographical information with photographs, text, statistics or other forms of data.

What matters is not simply the amount of information you collect, but what you can learn from it.

We want you to be critical about your sources, how you interpret the data and how you choose to represent it. Every map is selective. It reveals certain things while leaving others out.

We will introduce you to computational tools, including Python and AI-assisted workflows, to help you explore these possibilities.

But the tools are not the objective. The objective is to develop an interesting understanding of the city and, from that, a research question that can become the starting point for your design project.

## Slide 9 — Skills Module 01: Data Scraping the City

To support the first phase of your research, we will introduce our first skills module, which we call *Data Scraping the City*.

The idea is quite simple. Today, an enormous amount of information about cities exists online. People share their experiences, opinions, photographs and observations through digital platforms, social media and other sources.

We want to explore how we can collect some of this information and use it to understand the city from different perspectives.

You will learn how to work with Python to gather, organise and analyse data. We will also introduce AI-assisted programming, so you don't need to arrive with previous coding experience.

The examples you see here demonstrate how this information can be translated into maps that reveal patterns which might otherwise be difficult to recognise.

## Slide 10 — From Data to Cartographies of Affect

Of course, collecting data is only the first step. The more interesting question is what we can do with it.

We will look at different ways of analysing and representing information, from geographical maps to networks and more experimental visualisations.

For example, we might investigate how different groups perceive the same neighbourhood, where particular activities take place, or how people's attitudes towards certain urban spaces change over time.

We will work with tools such as Python and Rhino, but we also want you to experiment with your own methods of representation.

One important aspect of this module is learning to question the data itself. Online information is never a complete or neutral picture of the city. We need to understand who produces it, what it represents and what might be missing.

Ultimately, the aim is to turn all this information into a *Cartography of Affect* that helps you formulate your own research question about Berlin.
